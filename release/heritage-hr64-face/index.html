FIELD NOTES / HOW TO LISTEN

The receiver

This simulated receiver is an original design. Its light grey enamel panel, glass dial and nameplate are meant to mimic the style of military and commercial receivers of the 1950s and 1960s. The dial and S-meter are meant to indicate active frequencies for this project, not to measure the strength of each station’s RF reception.

Tuning and controls

The BAND switch selects 90 meters (3150–3500 KC), 60 meters (4700–5085 KC) or 49 meters (5850–6350 KC); KC means kilocycles, the period-appropriate name for kilohertz. Each range includes that band collection's recordings. Turn the BAND knob to a detent. Each band remembers where you last tuned. Grab and rotate the gain or tuning knob clockwise to increase its value. MAIN TUNING is speed sensitive. If turned slowly it travels about 5 KC per revolution, and a fast spin travels up to 100 KC per revolution. Arrow keys tune 0.1 KC and Page keys 5 KC. AF GAIN has bounded stops. A full-band slider and step buttons are under Alternative controls below the receiver.

On an iPhone or iPad, you will need to use your device's volume buttons to set listening level. AF GAIN adjusts the band noise; Apple does not let web pages set the loudness of the recordings themselves on iPhones and iPads.

The recordings

Currently, the receiver holds 670 recordings from 1969-1992: 113 on 90 meters, 363 on 60 meters and 194 on 49 meters. Shared frequencies use fixed clip-ID order and clip selectors on the listening desk to move between clips recorded on the same frequency.

Playing recordings: Play recording starts the tuned recording, and Restart plays it again from the beginning. Playback never advances to another recording on its own, whether a recording ends or fails to load. Loop recording, next to Restart, is off by default; its OFF/ON tag shows the setting. When it is on, the current recording starts again from the beginning each time it ends, and the line under the station title notes that Loop is on. Turning Loop on after a recording has ended does not start playback; press Replay recording. Turning it off during playback lets the recording finish its current pass and stop. Loop never moves to another recording and does not retry a recording that failed to load. The setting carries over as you tune to other recordings, and stays on until you turn it off or start a new blind session.

Static

Static defaults to on and is controlled by a switch below the listening desk. Recorded 75-second beds and randomly timed recorded crashes follow the current date in Eastern time (New York): December–February winter, March–May spring, June–August summer, September–November autumn. Winter is the baseline, spring/autumn are +1 dB, and summer is +2 dB. Spring temporarily uses autumn's bed and crash profile until I can locate or record a suitable spring audio file. The winter bed has mains-harmonic buzz reduction. These are seasonal impressions, not live weather or measured RF conditions.

Static is gentler during loading/buffering and fades completely out during archive playback. The recorded noise assets are bundled here; station recordings remain externally hosted. Band conditions run in the background and are not shown on the receiver face.

Noise recording credit: J. Christie / NA5B WebSDR, Washington, DC. Noise-only tails were cut from winter, summer, and autumn uploads marked Public Domain Mark 1.0 by the uploader. This label applies only to the noise recordings and is not a blanket rights clearance for the broadcast recordings.

Revealing a station

Station names stay concealed until the station’s identity is revealed by pressing the Reveal station button. After a reveal, a small map highlights the transmitter's country, republic or region as its borders stood on the date of that recording (borders: CShapes 2.0, Schvitz et al., CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; small areas: Natural Earth). Map data is not downloaded until the first reveal. The receiver has no input text boxes and keeps no log; it reads only the knobs, switches, buttons and lists on the page, and the season comes only from the current date. Research buttons are included with unaffiliated links to period-appropriate loggings and station/transmitter references. No documents are mirrored or embedded here.

Credits and sources

Recording credits: after you reveal a station, the line under the station’s details shows the name of the person who made or shared the recording, using the original source's own wording. "Recorded by" means the source names that person as the recordist. "Shared by" means the source says only that they contributed it. The line also provides the details for the archive that the recording comes from and, where the archive grants one, its license.

Map credits: period borders on the Reveal Station map come from CShapes 2.0 by Schvitz, Rüegger, Girardin, Cederman, Weidmann and Gleditsch (International Conflict Research, ETH Zurich), licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Citation: Schvitz, Guy, Seraina Rüegger, Luc Girardin, Lars-Erik Cederman, Nils Weidmann, and Kristian Skrede Gleditsch. 2022. "Mapping The International System, 1886-2017: The CShapes 2.0 Dataset." Journal of Conflict Resolution 66(1): 144–61.

Changes made for this receiver: only borders in effect between 1969 and 1992 are kept, the shapes are simplified for a small globe, coordinates are rounded, and outline direction is reordered for the drawing library. The modified borders file is shared under the same CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license. Small areas and modern stand-in shapes are made with Natural Earth (public domain). The map is drawn with d3-geo and d3-array (ISC license). The map shows a country, republic or region only, not a transmitter site.

Original audio plays directly from the underlying archival source; nothing is copied to this site. Each recording is used with its recordist's permission or under the applicable license shown in its credit.

About the Historic Shortwave Project

Why I built this

As a young shortwave listener in the 1960s and 1970s, I spent many evenings tuning across the bands, where distant and exotic stations drifted in and out through the static. It was a wonderful way to travel the world and experience other cultures without leaving home. Today those bands are mostly quiet. So many of the local stations that once filled them have moved to FM or to digital outlets, and tuning across the empty frequencies now leaves me a little sad.

Historic Shortwave is my attempt to bring back some of that experience. It plays recordings made between 1969 and 1992 on a simulated receiver, each one placed at the frequency where it was originally heard. You can tune across the 90, 60 and 49 meter bands, listen for a station identification, and then reveal the station’s details and location.

I vibe-coded this application with the help of Anthropic’s Claude Opus 5.5 AI model.
