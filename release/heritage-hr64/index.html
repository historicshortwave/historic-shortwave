FIELD NOTES / HOW TO LISTEN

A listening room, not a time machine.

This receiver is an original design. Its grey panel and nameplate recall military and commercial receivers of the 1950s and 1960s; it is not a product of, or endorsed by, any receiver maker. The dial and S-meter are simulated, not measured RF reception.

The BAND switch selects 90 meters (3150–3500 kHz), 60 meters (4700–5085 kHz) or 49 meters (5850–6350 kHz). Each range includes that collection's band-edge recordings; this does not redefine historical frequency allocations. Turn the BAND knob to a detent or tap a band number. Each band remembers where you last tuned. Grab and rotate the gain or tuning knob clockwise to increase its value. Fine tuning travels 12 kHz per revolution; coarse tuning travels 120 kHz. Arrow keys and the FINE step retain 0.1 kHz precision. Gain has bounded stops. Slider alternatives are available below the face.

The receiver holds 550 recordings from 1960-1989: 84 on 90 meters, 302 on 60 meters and 164 on 49 meters. Shared frequencies use fixed clip-ID order and neutral date/clip selectors on the receiver face.

Playing recordings: Play recording starts the tuned recording, and Restart plays it again from the beginning. Playback never advances to another recording on its own, whether a recording ends or fails to load. Loop recording, next to Restart, is off by default; its OFF/ON tag shows the setting. When it is on, the current recording starts again from the beginning each time it ends, and the line under the station title notes that Loop is on. Turning Loop on after a recording has ended does not start playback; press Replay recording. Turning it off during playback lets the recording finish its current pass and stop. Loop never moves to another recording and does not retry a recording that failed to load. The setting carries over as you tune to other recordings, and stays on until you turn it off or start a new blind session.

Static defaults to on and is controlled below the listening desk. Recorded 75-second beds and randomly timed recorded crashes follow today's date in America/New_York: December–February winter, March–May spring, June–August summer, September–November autumn. Winter is the baseline, spring/autumn are +1 dB, and summer is +2 dB. Spring temporarily uses autumn's bed and crash profile. The winter bed has mains-harmonic buzz reduction. These are seasonal impressions, not live weather or measured RF conditions.

Static is gentler during loading/buffering and fades completely out during archive playback. The recorded noise assets are bundled here; station recordings remain externally hosted. Band conditions run in the background and are not shown on the receiver face.

Noise recording credit: J. Christie / NA5B WebSDR, Washington, DC. Noise-only tails were cut from winter, summer, and autumn uploads marked Public Domain Mark 1.0 by the uploader. This label is not a blanket rights clearance for broadcast recordings.

Station names stay concealed until revealed. After a reveal, a small map highlights the transmitter's country, republic or region as its borders stood on the recording date (borders: CShapes 2.0, Schvitz et al., CC BY-NC-SA 4.0; small areas: Natural Earth). Map data is not downloaded until the first reveal. The receiver has no text boxes and keeps no log; it reads only the knobs, switches, buttons and lists on the page, and the season comes only from the date. Research buttons open the owner's original external destinations; no documents are mirrored or embedded here.

Recording credits: after you reveal a station, the line under its details names the person who made or shared the recording, using the source's own wording. "Recorded by" means the source names that person as the recordist. "Shared by" means the source says only that they contributed it. Where the source names no one, the line says so; names are never guessed. The line also gives the archive the recording comes from and, where the archive grants one, its license. The broadcasts themselves belong to the original stations.

Map credits: period borders on the Reveal Station map come from CShapes 2.0 by Schvitz, Rüegger, Girardin, Cederman, Weidmann and Gleditsch (International Conflict Research, ETH Zurich), licensed under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Citation: Schvitz, Guy, Seraina Rüegger, Luc Girardin, Lars-Erik Cederman, Nils Weidmann, and Kristian Skrede Gleditsch. 2022. "Mapping The International System, 1886-2017: The CShapes 2.0 Dataset." Journal of Conflict Resolution 66(1): 144–61.

Changes made for this receiver: only borders in effect between 1960 and 1989 are kept, the shapes are simplified for a small globe, coordinates are rounded, and outline direction is reordered for the drawing library. The modified borders file is shared under the same CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 license. Small areas and modern stand-in shapes are made with Natural Earth (public domain). The map is drawn with d3-geo and d3-array (ISC license). Type: Archivo and IBM Plex Mono (SIL Open Font License 1.1), served from this site rather than a font service, so loading the page sends nothing to a third party. The map shows a country, republic or region only, not a transmitter site.

Original audio plays directly from the Internet Archive; nothing is copied to this site. Each recording is used with its recordist's permission or under the license shown in its credit. Source URLs and technical inspection may expose station identity.

ABOUT THIS PROJECT

About the Historic Shortwave Project

Why I built this

As a young shortwave listener in the 1960’s and 1970’s I spent many evenings tuning the shortwave radio bands, where a great many distant and exotic stations came and went through the static. It was a great way to virtually travel the world and experience other cultures. Today the shortwave radio bands are mostly quiet. In particular, so many of the local stations that once filled them have moved to FM and to digital outlets, and tuning across those empty frequencies now leaves me a little sad.

Historic Shortwave is my attempt to bring back some of that original experience. It plays audio recordings made between 1960 and 1989 on a simulated receiver, each at the frequency where it was originally heard, so you can tune across the 90, 60 and 49 meter bands, listen for an identification and then reveal the station’s details and location.

Thank you to the recordists
